# NegotiatorGrid — Research Context

## Background

The autonomous agent economy is projected to reach $4.4 trillion, with agents increasingly handling complex workflows including financial transactions [1]. Infrastructure projects like Kite AI [2] and protocols like x402 [3] enable agents to discover services and execute payments on-chain. However, these systems operate on a fundamentally one-shot model: a service advertises a fixed price, the agent pays or walks away. The recent SoK on blockchain agent-to-agent payments [3] systematizes this into a four-stage lifecycle — Discovery, Authorization, Execution, and Accounting — but identifies that no stage accounts for how payment terms are *formed* when they aren't fixed upfront. Current infrastructure gives agents wallets and payment rails, but not the ability to *bargain*.

Multi-round price negotiation between autonomous agents is an explicitly recognized open problem. The SoK paper states: *"agent-mediated interactions can involve iterative or multi-round negotiation over price, volume, or service scope… lifecycle models should account for how payable conditions are formed across interactions"* [3]. Meanwhile, benchmarks like AgenticPay [4] demonstrate that frontier LLMs *can* negotiate — Claude Opus 4.5 closes deals in 3.7 rounds with a 100% success rate across 111 negotiation tasks. Yet the same benchmarks reveal that LLMs negotiate poorly without guidance: they anchor at extremes regardless of context [5], over-cooperate toward Pareto-optimal outcomes at the expense of individual rationality [6], and are exploitable by simple behavioral manipulation tactics that shift payoffs by 20% [7]. The gap is not whether agents can negotiate, but whether they can negotiate *well* — with strategic rationality, formal guarantees, and verifiable outcomes.

NegotiatorGrid fills this gap by combining three advances. First, we adopt the hybrid LLM-optimizer architecture from ASTRA [8], where an LLM infers opponent preferences while a game-theoretic engine (NegMAS aspiration curves + Nash Bargaining Solution) generates provably Pareto-efficient offers — demonstrated to improve outcomes by 3.59 points over pure LLM approaches (p < 0.001). Second, we settle negotiated prices through Kite's x402 protocol with cryptographic deal enforcement, addressing the atomicity gap identified by A402 [9] through escrow contracts and session key spending limits bound to the agreed price. Third, we expose NegotiatorGrid's negotiation capabilities as MCP tools for dynamic discovery, following the production design patterns for identity propagation, adaptive tool budgeting, and structured error recovery identified in enterprise MCP deployments [10]. The result is the first protocol that bridges the formation of payment terms — through formal bilateral negotiation — with their on-chain enforcement and settlement.

## Judge Q&A Ammo

### Q: "What's the academic basis for this?"

The SoK on blockchain A2A payments [3] — the first systematization of this field, published April 2026 — explicitly identifies "Formation: negotiating payable terms across interactions" as one of four open future directions. The Agent Economy paper [1] defines a five-layer architecture where NegotiatorGrid operates at the Economic & Settlement layer. AgenticPay [4] benchmarks 111 negotiation tasks across 5 LLMs, proving that LLM-mediated negotiation is feasible with frontier models (100% deal rate for Claude Opus 4.5 and GPT-5.2). NegotiatorGrid is building the protocol that these papers independently identify as missing.

### Q: "Is x402 secure for negotiated prices?"

x402 has four documented failure modes: weak intent binding, misuse under valid authorization, payment-service decoupling, and limited accountability [3]. The A402 paper [9] further identifies that x402 "fails to enforce end-to-end atomicity across service execution, payment, and result delivery" — a provider can receive payment and not deliver. NegotiatorGrid mitigates these through: (1) a `deal_hash` (keccak256 of agreed terms) that cryptographically binds the negotiated price to the payment session, preventing replay and mismatching; (2) a Nash Validator that is deterministic arithmetic — not LLM-based — so it cannot be subverted by prompt injection [Whispers of Wealth, arXiv:2601.22569]; (3) SLA terms encoded in Kite's on-chain SLA contracts with automatic penalties for violations; and (4) session keys with `valueLimit` = negotiated price and `allowedRecipients` = [counterparty only], enforced by the AA smart contract.

### Q: "Why game theory instead of just LLM negotiation?"

Three independent findings demand it. First, the Game-Theoretic LLM paper [6] showed that without structured game-theoretic workflows, LLMs achieve Nash equilibria less than 10% of the time in strategic games — with workflows, 90-100%. Second, the LLM Rationalis paper [5] found that "LLMs systematically anchor at extremes of the ZOPA regardless of context" and that "the ability of LLMs to negotiate does not improve with better models." Third, ASTRA [8] proved that an LP-optimized offer generator produces outcomes 3.59 points higher than pure LLM generation (t = 8.55, p < 0.001). The pattern across all three: LLMs are great at preference inference and natural language, terrible at optimization. NegotiatorGrid's architecture puts the LLM where it excels (opponent modeling, NL communication) and a game-theoretic engine where optimization is needed (aspiration curves, Nash Bargaining Solution, Pareto-optimal offer generation).

### Q: "Why account abstraction?"

Agent Contracts [arXiv:2601.08815] formalizes how agents need resource-bounded execution with conservation laws ensuring delegated budgets respect parent constraints. Kite's ERC-4337 AA wallet implements this: a single on-chain account where multiple agents operate via session keys, each constrained by smart contract rules. Critically for NegotiatorGrid, `addSessionKeyRule(valueLimit, dailyLimit, validUntil, allowedRecipients, metadata)` lets us set the spending cap to exactly the negotiated price, lock the counterparty as the only valid recipient, and store the deal hash as metadata — all cryptographically enforced. An EOA wallet can't do this. The Binding Agent ID paper [arXiv:2512.17538] goes further: zkVM-based Code-Level Authentication can prove the exact binary that validated our negotiation offers, giving counterparties cryptographic assurance that the game-theoretic guardrails weren't bypassed.

### Q: "What about MCP security?"

The MCP Security Threats paper [arXiv:2503.23278] identifies 16 threat scenarios across four attacker types. The most relevant to NegotiatorGrid's dynamic discovery are: tool poisoning (malicious logic in tool descriptions that LLMs trust), cross-server shadowing (attacker's tool intercepts legitimate calls), and indirect prompt injection (adversarial text in opponent offers biases the LLM). We mitigate with three layers: (1) structural separation — the `natural_language` field in offers is untrusted display text; the structured `offer.price` field is what the protocol enforces; (2) deterministic validation — the Nash Validator is arithmetic, not LLM-based, so prompt injection cannot bypass it; (3) production patterns from [10] — CABP identity propagation verifies counterparty DIDs, SERF structured errors prevent hallucinated recovery, ATBA adaptive budgeting prevents timeout-based manipulation. Additionally, 97.1% of MCP tool descriptions contain quality defects [arXiv:2602.14878] — NegotiatorGrid's tools follow the six-component rubric (purpose, parameters, returns, side effects, errors, usage context) with full typed JSON schemas.

### Q: "How do you prevent agents from colluding?"

The Inter-Agent Trust Models paper [11] evaluates six trust mechanisms: Brief, Claim, Proof, Stake, Reputation, and Constraint. It recommends "trustless-by-default architectures anchored in Proof and Stake to gate high-impact actions." NegotiatorGrid implements: (1) **Proof** — every deal produces a `deal_hash` (signed by both parties) committed on-chain, creating a cryptographic record of the agreed terms; (2) **Stake** — the buyer's funds are locked in an escrow/session key with `valueLimit` = negotiated price before the deal executes; (3) **Reputation** — Kite's on-chain reputation system tracks deal completion rates, SLA compliance, and negotiation outcomes; agents with poor histories get lower trust scores that feed into opponent modeling; (4) **Constraint** — the Nash Validator bounds all offers within the ZOPA, preventing agents from colluding on prices outside the legitimate bargaining range. The `welfare_attestation` block in every deal (GlobalScore = γ^(t-1) × (D + 4·rb·rs·W + E), from AgenticPay [4]) provides a quantitative on-chain record of whether the deal was Pareto-efficient — systematically unfair deals produce low GlobalScores that trigger reputation penalties.

### Q: "How does this differ from Agentic Markets (ETHDenver 3rd place)?"

[Agentic Markets](https://github.com/kamalbuilds/agentic-markets) built a multi-chain marketplace where agents discover tasks, propose prices, and settle payments across 4 chains via 57 MCP tools. It's impressive infrastructure, but the "negotiation" is a one-shot propose-accept pattern — Agent B proposes a price, Agent A accepts or rejects. There is no multi-round bargaining, no aspiration-based concession strategy, no opponent modeling from offer history, and no game-theoretic validation that the agreed price is near the Nash Bargaining Solution. NegotiatorGrid adds four capabilities they lack:

| Capability | Agentic Markets | NegotiatorGrid |
|-----------|----------------|---------------|
| Multi-round bargaining | One-shot propose/accept | SAOP with configurable rounds, deadline pressure, aspiration curves |
| Opponent modeling | None | FrequencyLinearUFunModel + ASTRA stance classification (Generous/Neutral/Greedy) |
| Game-theoretic validation | None | Nash Validator checks every offer against NBS, aspiration bounds, and ZOPA |
| Cryptographic deal enforcement | On-chain payment only | `deal_hash` = keccak256(agreed terms) → `addSessionKeyRule(metadata=deal_hash)` with per-deal spending limits |
| Welfare attestation | None | `GlobalScore = γ^(t-1) × (D + 4·rb·rs·W + E)` committed on-chain |
| Dynamic concession | Fixed price | `u(t) = 1 - t^exponent` where exponent adapts to opponent stance |

In short: Agentic Markets solved discovery + payment. NegotiatorGrid solves what happens *between* discovery and payment — the negotiation itself.
